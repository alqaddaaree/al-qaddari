In recent years, many Muslims have taken to social media platforms—YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok—with the claim of spreading da’wah. Some defend this practice by quoting scholars, saying that such platforms are opportunities to convey knowledge. Others argue that avoiding them is ghuluww (extremism) or jumood (rigidity).

We must address this matter clearly, based on Islamic principles.

#### 1\. The Reality of These Platforms

The platforms in question are filled with haram: indecent images, immoral speech, vain entertainment, and endless fitan. They are designed to be addictive, capturing attention and subtly influencing behavior. Exposure to these platforms is harmful, both spiritually and mentally.

Many scholars who speak of “opportunities” on these platforms do so from principle, not from lived experience of the current level of filth and deception. Their advice does not mean full-time immersion in these haram environments. The reality today is far worse than what they could have imagined.

#### 3\. Quality Over Quantity

Some justify social media presence as “not letting the kuffar take over the space.” This reasoning reverses true Salafi priorities. The Salafi method values **quality over quantity** : a small, sincere circle of students who act upon knowledge is far superior to thousands exposed to fitan, who may neither benefit nor act correctly. Visibility is not equivalent to benefit.

#### 4\. Informed Decisions

It is often claimed that those who avoid social media are extreme or outdated. This is a misrepresentation. One can be fully aware of technology, algorithms, and digital traps—and still choose not to engage with platforms rife with fitan. Rejecting haram is not extremism; it is principle.

Being tech-savvy or aware of modern platforms’ mechanisms strengthens the decision to avoid them. One is not blind or stubborn, but discerning and responsible.

#### 5\. Practical Alternatives

Da’wah can be spread safely without these haram platforms:

  * Own websites or blogs for articles and lectures.
  * Private channels or messaging groups (WhatsApp, Signal, Telegram) for controlled distribution.
  * Audio and video distributed as downloadable files.
  * Physical halaqas, circles, and printed materials.
  * Islamic apps or software controlled by you.



The focus must remain on **safeguarding barakah and the integrity of knowledge** , rather than chasing followers in environments filled with fitan.

* * *

**Conclusion:**  
Using social media platforms as a da’wah tool may seem attractive, but it carries enormous spiritual risks. Scholars’ statements must be understood in context. Avoiding these platforms is neither ghuluw nor jumood—it is adherence to the Shariah and protection of oneself and the students of knowledge. True benefit lies in quality, safety, and sincerity, not in numbers or visibility.
